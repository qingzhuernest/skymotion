Sundial lab

Sundials turn the Sun's direction into a time reading. Their gnomons are parallel to Earth's axis, unlike the vertical gnomon used for shadow lengths. The equatorial gnomon passes through the dial so both faces can be used. Both instruments share the current location, date, and local apparent solar time.

01 · Courtyards and plazas

Horizontal sundial

The dial is horizontal; the gnomon points toward the celestial pole of the observer's hemisphere.

Sundial reading

Hour lines are generally unevenly spaced and depend on latitude. The gnomon's elevation equals the absolute latitude. Recalibrate the instrument if you move it to another latitude.

How to build and install it

Use cardboard for a horizontal base and a straight rod as the gnomon. Align it with true north–south and set its elevation as shown above, pointing toward your hemisphere's celestial pole. Draw hour lines for the current latitude; do not copy the equally spaced marks of an ordinary clock.

02 · Parks and astronomy classes

Equatorial sundial

The dial is parallel to Earth's equatorial plane; the gnomon is perpendicular to the dial.

Sundial reading

Adjacent hour lines differ by 15°. As solar declination changes, illumination switches between the two faces, whose hour marks are mirrored. Near the equinoxes, sunlight is nearly parallel to the dial, making it difficult to read.

How to build and install it

Use a double-sided cardboard disk with a perpendicular rod through its center. Draw hour lines 15° apart on each face, reversing their left–right order. Install the disk at the tilt shown above so the rod is parallel to Earth's axis; do not lay the disk horizontally.

The 3D instrument uses the same geometry; Dial reading and installation retains orthographic views. Gnomon length is adjustable (default horizontal length 0.18 m; equatorial length 0.18 m on each side), and dial radius is 0.32 m. Arrows show a shadow tip outside the dial. Align installations with true north–south; correct a magnetic compass for magnetic declination. Directions at the poles are not unique, so the selected meridian is used as a reference. Shadows are calculated from ray–dial intersections; line width and soft edges aid visibility without simulating actual penumbra, weather, or terrain obstruction.

Think about it: why might a sundial and your phone show different times?

A sundial reads local apparent solar time. A phone usually shows its time zone's clock time. Longitude relative to the zone's central meridian and the changing equation of time both cause differences. This model uses a fixed UTC offset and does not automatically apply daylight saving time.

Try it: keep the date fixed and change longitude from 120°E to 105°E. Compare clock readings at the same local apparent solar time. Then keep the location fixed and change the date to explore the equation of time.

  1. Compare seasons at a fixed time:In the Sun view, enable fixed-time date observations. Drag the annual date slider or play dates to compare the Sun and gnomon shadow. Overlay annual paths at the same time and link the analemma below. This keeps local apparent solar time fixed, not Beijing clock time.
  2. Explore the analemma:Open the analemma from the Sun's linked diagrams. Observe the annual figure eight at a fixed clock time, then compare a fixed apparent solar time. The analemma can use its own time; linked daily observations use the main lab's apparent solar time. The UTC offset is fixed and excludes daylight saving time.
  3. Compare seasons:Keep Beijing selected. Compare the June and December solstices, or drag the annual month slider to examine noon altitude and daylight duration. Seasons primarily result from Earth's axial tilt changing solar altitude and daylight duration, not from changing Earth–Sun distance.
  4. Compare latitudes:Keep the date fixed and move the latitude from the equator toward the North Pole. Observe the path's tilt, polar day, and polar night.
  5. Compare rise/set azimuths:Compare sunrise and sunset azimuths; click a result to jump to that time. Read the notes for polar day, polar night, and the poles.
  6. Understand shadows:Choose Shadow plan view and drag the time slider. Under Display aids → Shadows and shadow-tip paths, enlarge the ground, show the daily tip path, and click it to change time. A vertical gnomon's shadow on level ground points away from the Sun. Gnomon height affects shadow length, not the observer's elevation.
  7. Sundials in everyday life:Open the standalone Sundial lab from the Sun's linked diagrams. Drag time or the shadow on the dial, or play a day, to compare both instruments. Change the gnomon length to examine shadows and time readings. Sundial gnomons are parallel to Earth's axis and read local apparent solar time, which differs from a phone's clock time.
  8. Look up:Choose Look up to see the Sun, Moon, and five planets from a ground observer's perspective. Drag to turn or click a body's name to face it. The sky is an orthographic projection of the forward hemisphere; bodies below the horizon or behind you are described in the list. Planet positions shown in daylight do not imply naked-eye visibility.
  9. Connect with Earth:Switch between the globe and world map to examine the subsolar point and day–night regions. Click the map to choose a location. The linked diagram shows Earth's axis, the observer, and the local horizon plane.
  10. Understand Moon–planet conjunctions:In Moon phases, enable the conjunction demonstration and select a planet. Compare spherical angular separation and altitude at the same location and time. Find the closest date in the next 30 days at that fixed time. A conjunction does not mean the planet and Moon are close in space, and small angular separation does not guarantee easy naked-eye viewing.
  11. Explore Moon phases:Choose Moon phases to explore illumination and Sun–Earth–Moon geometry. Advance dates while keeping the observation time fixed to compare phases and sky positions. Choose Moon sky for its apparent motion, or overlay the Moon in the Sun view. The phase cycle is about 29.53 days, not one calendar month. The Moon can also appear in the daytime sky.

Time is local apparent solar time: the Sun transits near 12:00 (exactly 12:00 in the idealized experiment), rather than your phone's time-zone clock. Ephemeris mode uses astronomical calculations. Seasonal buttons select solstice and equinox local dates for the current year; their names and comparison-line labels follow Northern Hemisphere seasons, which are reversed in the Southern Hemisphere. The idealized experiment fixes solar declination to study latitude and seasons; selected date and UTC are not used. Comparison curves use ideal declinations of 0° and ±23.44°. Lunar calculations include topocentric parallax, placing the observer on the ground rather than at Earth's center. Both bodies rise or set when their centers cross the geometric horizon; atmospheric refraction and terrain obstruction are omitted. Lunar illumination is the lit fraction of the disk seen from Earth, using a geocentric apparent-phase approximation. “Near” a principal phase does not identify its exact instant. Eclipse obstruction and brightness changes are not simulated. Elapsed durations in ephemeris mode use UTC intervals and differ slightly from apparent-solar-time reading intervals. Phase icons use a fixed orientation rather than the locally observed bright-limb orientation, which depends on hemisphere and observation time. A day's maximum altitude can occur at the 00:00 or 24:00 boundary and need not coincide with lunar upper transit. A lunar direction and altitude do not guarantee naked-eye visibility; new moons and daytime conditions can make viewing difficult. Some azimuths are not uniquely defined at the poles or zenith.
