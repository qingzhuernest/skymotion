Starting points and prerequisites

This guide supports classroom teaching and independent inquiry. Start with a testable question, use the feature manual to find controls, then fix conditions, vary a quantity, record evidence and explain the outcome.

Four conventions to state before an experiment

  • Location is a physical condition; rotating the camera changes only the view. Move the observer using coordinates, location sliders or the explicit observer-move tool.
  • Altitude is 0° at the geometric horizon and 90° at the zenith. Azimuth runs clockwise from north: east 90°, south 180°, west 270°.
  • The current clock, annual analemma sample clock, 3D UTC time and independent Earthrise flight controls are not one interchangeable clock.
  • Real-date observation, ideal solar-declination experiments and the circular Earthrise lesson have different assumptions; name the mode before comparing results.

Planning a lesson

Example for an approximately forty-minute lesson

  1. Introduce an everyday question and collect predictions, such as whether noon shadows are longer in winter.
  2. State place, date, time basis and views, then demonstrate changing one quantity at a time.
  3. Assign recorder, operator and explainer roles; use one shared worksheet for at least three comparisons.
  4. Require a second view or reading to support the explanation rather than merely describing motion.
  5. Discuss and transfer: explain a new latitude, the opposite hemisphere or a site near the date line.
  6. Finish with a conclusion, evidence, controls and one factor omitted by the model.

Use customization as a teaching tool

Customization should serve a question. Decide which conditions to compare before adding cities, date curves, clocks, references, measurements or ground objects; do not add every tool merely to fill the screen.

  1. Keep the baseline, then choose one place at different clocks, one place on different dates, or different places at one instant.
  2. Use linked highlighting to follow one reference across views; the active pane is marked by its outline.
  3. Name references and saves using conditions, such as 40N, near solstice and fixed apparent solar time.
  4. To keep a full experiment, include conditions and objects in the saved-layout form, and retain a written record describing local-device storage.

Investigation 1: How does the Sun move through a day?

Inquiry question

At one place on one date, how do solar altitude and azimuth vary through the day?

Fix these conditions first

Choose Beijing or approximately 40°N, 116°E and a date near the Northern Hemisphere summer solstice. Use real-date observation, pause playback and keep the place and date fixed. The daily timeline uses local apparent solar time.

Procedure and recording

  1. Open the spatial Sky & shadows view and add Daily altitude. Predict when the Sun will be highest.
  2. Set 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00 and 18:00. Record altitude, azimuth and whether the Sun is above the horizon.
  3. Move near a zero crossing of the altitude curve and check the rise/set events. Clicking an event marker jumps to its time.
  4. Play the daily clock and watch the diurnal path. Pause and add time markers for two positions to compare.

What to look for

Solar altitude rises and falls, and the geometric horizon is altitude 0°. Apparent solar noon corresponds to upper culmination, but the Sun must not be assumed due south at every place and date.

How to explain it

The daily change mainly reflects Earth’s rotation. The path is apparent motion in the observer’s sky; screen distance from the Sun to a rod is not the physical Earth–Sun distance.

Check understanding

Give one piece of evidence from each view and explain why the dashed path below the horizon is not a physical underground orbit.

Investigation 2: Seasonal changes at one place

Inquiry question

Why do noon altitude, daylight duration and sunrise direction vary seasonally at one place?

Fix these conditions first

Fix latitude, longitude and apparent solar noon. Use real dates near equinoxes and solstices. Exact event times follow the ephemeris; a fixed calendar date is not the exact seasonal instant every year.

Procedure and recording

  1. Combine Earth & daylight, Daily altitude, Rise/set bearings and Axis & horizon.
  2. Set dates around the two equinoxes and two solstices; record noon altitude, rise/set directions and daylight duration.
  3. Keep one season as a date comparison curve, then change the shared date to compare another.
  4. Repeat at the same absolute latitude in the Southern Hemisphere and compare seasonal patterns.

What to look for

The daily path and rise/set directions change with date. Northern and Southern Hemisphere seasons are opposite; equatorial, tropical and high-latitude patterns differ.

How to explain it

Axial tilt produces changing solar declination through the orbit. Upper-culmination altitude follows 90° minus the absolute difference between latitude and declination; this alone does not state whether the Sun is north or south.

Check understanding

Connect the subsolar latitude to the curve peak and name the conditions that must remain fixed.

Investigation 3: Latitude and the day–night pattern

Inquiry question

How do solar paths and daylight duration differ with latitude on one date?

Fix these conditions first

Fix date and longitude. First compare the same apparent solar clock reading; investigate a shared UTC instant separately.

Procedure and recording

  1. Use the different-places comparison to add equatorial, middle-latitude and high-latitude sites.
  2. Explicitly choose equal apparent solar time and record noon altitudes and rise/set behavior.
  3. Scrub one site’s latitude and watch its automatic name, curve, position and legend update together.
  4. Investigate a high-latitude date with no rise/set events and use the altitude curve to distinguish continuous daylight from continuous darkness.

What to look for

High latitudes can produce continuous daylight, continuous darkness or a horizon tangency. Missing event markers can be valid, and geographic north is not uniquely defined at a pole.

How to explain it

Altitude depends on latitude, declination and hour angle. Latitude changes the local horizon’s orientation relative to the axis; screen position alone is not an altitude measurement.

Check understanding

Describe ordinary crossings, continuous daylight, continuous darkness and tangency, using the curve rather than one image as evidence.

Investigation 4: The same clock reading versus the same instant

Inquiry question

If Beijing and another city both show 12:00, are they observing the same instant?

Fix these conditions first

Use real-date observation. Fix the baseline place, date and clock and add a city at a clearly different longitude without moving the baseline observer.

Procedure and recording

  1. Choose equal apparent solar time and compare altitudes at equal solar clock readings.
  2. Switch to one UTC instant and examine the comparison city’s apparent solar time and possible date rollover.
  3. Move longitude to either side of the date line and record dates and altitudes while keeping latitude fixed.
  4. Select a city and follow its highlighted references across views; use Make baseline only when you intend to move the primary observer.

What to look for

Equal apparent solar clock readings generally refer to different UTC instants. One UTC instant can have different local solar times and even different dates.

How to explain it

Longitude controls orientation relative to the solar meridian. Apparent solar time is not necessarily a phone’s civil time; administrative time zones, daylight saving and the equation of time are distinct issues.

Check understanding

Make a two-column record explicitly labeled fixed UTC and fixed apparent solar time rather than simply same time.

Investigation 5: Rod height, shadow length and shadow-tip paths

Inquiry question

Why does a shadow change direction and length? Does a taller rod change solar altitude?

Fix these conditions first

Fix place, date and the ground, starting with a vertical rod. The horizontal-ground shadow-length relation applies when the Sun is above the horizon and the rod is vertical.

Procedure and recording

  1. Open the overhead shadow view and inspect shadow length and direction at 09:00, 12:00 and 15:00.
  2. Show the shadow-tip path and change display magnification, distinguishing magnification from rod height.
  3. Build two vertical rods of different heights with explicit positions and constraints, then compare using measurement tools.
  4. Keep a reference and change the date to compare paths; separately record display limits near the horizon.

What to look for

A shadow points opposite the Sun’s horizontal projection. For vertical rods at one solar altitude, shadow length is proportional to rod height; at night no direct-sunlight ground shadow should appear.

How to explain it

For altitude h and vertical height H, horizontal shadow length is L=H/tan(h). The length becomes very large near h=0°; canvas clipping does not physically end a shadow.

Check understanding

State what rod height, camera zoom and display magnification each change, and explain why a clipped shadow cannot validate the formula.

Investigation 6: Why can a sundial tell time?

Inquiry question

Why do horizontal and equatorial sundials use different hour markings?

Fix these conditions first

Open the sundial controls or the sundial URL. Fix place and date and distinguish solar-time markings from interpretations using a fixed civil offset.

Procedure and recording

  1. Inspect horizontal and equatorial versions and identify the gnomon, dial plane and rotation axis.
  2. Pause at several whole hours and compare shadow lines with hour markings, recording cases without a valid intersection.
  3. Change latitude alone and observe changes in the horizontal dial and gnomon orientation.
  4. Change the season and inspect which side of the equatorial plane is illuminated and whether direct sunlight is available.

What to look for

Horizontal hour markings are generally unequal; an equatorial plane represents hour angle around the rotation axis. The markings cannot be interchanged between designs.

How to explain it

The apparatus geometry determines ray–plane intersections. Solar time, civil time and longitude corrections differ, and a fixed offset does not automatically handle daylight saving.

Check understanding

Give at least two reasons why a physical sundial may disagree with a phone clock.

Investigation 7: Moon phases and lunar rising and setting

Inquiry question

Are lunar phase changes the same process as daily motion across the sky?

Fix these conditions first

Choose Moon mode and a real date. First hold the clock fixed and step through dates to study phase, then hold a date fixed to study daily position.

Procedure and recording

  1. Combine phase relationships, illumination, lunar rise/set and the sky view.
  2. Record illumination, orbital phase position and the visible bright-limb orientation on successive dates.
  3. Return to one date, scrub through the day and compare lunar altitude with rise/set events.
  4. Drag a rise/set marker and observe the date search, identifying the changed date rather than interpreting the interaction as free compass rotation.

What to look for

Phase mainly reflects Sun–Earth–Moon geometry. A calendar day may contain only a rise or only a set; the Moon does not recur at a fixed clock time each day.

How to explain it

Bright-limb orientation also depends on local orientation and viewpoint. Teaching-scale sizes and distances in a phase diagram cannot establish eclipses.

Check understanding

Use separate records to explain monthly phase variation and daily altitude change, naming the controls held fixed in each.

Investigation 8: Planet–Moon comparisons and angular separation

Inquiry question

Do two objects that look close in the sky have to be close in space?

Fix these conditions first

Fix place and real date and choose the Moon and a planet. Angular separation is an angle between sight lines, not a distance in kilometers.

Procedure and recording

  1. Open Moon & planets or Looking up, select a planet and verify whether both objects are above the horizon.
  2. Step dates and record separation; when searching, check the observer, time basis and date range first.
  3. Keep markers or position records, and do not move the actual observer while merely turning the camera.
  4. Check spatial positions in the 3D Solar System and distinguish projected proximity from physical distance.

What to look for

Small angular separation means similar sky directions. Horizon position, darkness and real observing conditions still affect visibility.

How to explain it

The local-sky display projects viewing directions, while the 3D Solar System is a scale-adjusted spatial illustration. They complement each other but their displayed distances are not interchangeable.

Check understanding

Explain why a search result does not guarantee naked-eye visibility and list additional observing conditions to check.

Investigation 9: Why can the analemma look like a figure eight?

Inquiry question

Why does the Sun recorded at one civil clock time each day not trace only a north–south line?

Fix these conditions first

Use real dates and a fixed place. Set the annual sampling basis to a fixed civil clock with an explicit UTC offset. Annual sample time and the current observation clock are separate controls.

Procedure and recording

  1. Open Analemma and inspect vertical extent, horizontal variation and the current date marker.
  2. Change only the fixed annual sampling time and inspect the resulting position in the local sky.
  3. Switch to apparent solar time or link to the lower apparent-solar clock and compare the horizontal equation-of-time component.
  4. In the idealized cause experiment, vary axial tilt and orbital eccentricity separately, then compare with real-date mode.

What to look for

Fixed civil-clock sampling combines declination changes with the equation of time. Fixed apparent solar time removes the corresponding hour-angle variation, although different projections can still produce geometric curvature.

How to explain it

Axial tilt and a noncircular orbit affect the direction at a fixed civil clock time. The ideal model isolates causes; it is not merely a different visual style for the real ephemeris.

Check understanding

Record sampling basis, UTC offset, sample clock, location and model mode; omitting any of them makes reproduction difficult.

Investigation 10: Why does Earth rise and set above the Moon?

Inquiry question

Is orbital Earthrise equivalent to seeing Earth rise daily from a fixed lunar site?

Fix these conditions first

In Moon mode open Earthrise. This independent circular-orbit lesson controls orbital angle, flight speed and Sun–Earth–Moon configuration separately from the lower real-date ephemeris clock.

Procedure and recording

  1. Choose orbital observation and Watch Earthrise, comparing the orbit diagram with the astronaut view.
  2. Pause and change orbital position, explaining how the lunar limb hides or reveals Earth.
  3. Change altitude and compare horizon depression and emergence; use Look toward Earth if Earth leaves the view.
  4. Switch to a fixed surface site and change the configuration, comparing Earth’s direction with its illuminated fraction.

What to look for

Orbital motion changes the sight line relative to the lunar limb. At a fixed near-side site in this synchronous-rotation model, Earth does not rise and set daily, although its illuminated fraction can vary.

How to explain it

The lesson omits lunar libration, terrain and precise mission trajectories. Animation speed is not the physical orbital period.

Check understanding

Sketch the observer–limb–Earth sight line and distinguish turning the camera, moving in orbit and changing illumination geometry.

Investigation 11: Explain apparent motion using a spatial model

Inquiry question

How are heliocentric positions, Earth-based directions and local-sky readings connected?

Fix these conditions first

Open 3D Sky and fix a date, noting the UTC label. Body sizes, planetary orbital distances and Moon–Earth distances use different teaching scales.

Procedure and recording

  1. Select Earth and another planet in the overview and distinguish physical values in the facts panel from displayed sizes.
  2. Switch to Sun–Earth–Moon relationships and identify sunlight, lunar illumination and Earth’s orientation.
  3. Open the diagram comparison or local observer view and cross-check directions at the same UTC instant.
  4. Use reference planes, distance lines, angle arcs or labels as evidence, checking model and display limits for additional populations.

What to look for

Camera rotation does not change physical positions. Display distances can be compressed while physical vectors and readings retain their stated units.

How to explain it

Converting spatial positions to local sky directions requires the observer, Earth orientation and projection; separation between rendered points is not itself sky angular separation.

Check understanding

Identify one deliberately exaggerated display quantity and one quantity computed from the ephemeris, explaining their different purposes.

Assessing an explanation

DimensionEvidence of success
Explicit conditionsAnother learner can reproduce place, date, clock and mode
Reliable dataUnits and time basis are stated; screen distances are not treated as physical distances
Causal explanationTrends are connected to rotation, tilt, sight lines or projection
Model awarenessThe learner can explain absent events, poles, clipping, teaching scales or omitted weather

Have students exchange written records and reproduce each other’s experiments before discussing discrepancies. Browser saves do not sync across devices, so sharing also requires conditions, screenshots or available reference export files.
